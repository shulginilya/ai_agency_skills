# Change → issue

## The title you were given

Requested title: `$ARGUMENTS`

Everything typed after the command is the issue title, raw and unsplit — that is
why the source reads `\$ARGUMENTS` here rather than a named argument: a title is
free prose, and a positional split would hand you only its first word. When
nothing was typed, the backticks above are empty. Check which case you are in
before doing anything else.

Invoked as, quoting therefore optional:

```
/change-to-issue feat(skills): change-to-issue takes an optional issue title
/change-to-issue "fix(validator): reject skill dirs whose name differs from frontmatter"
/change-to-issue feat(hooks): add PostToolUse audit hook   # two pieces of work in the
                                                           # tree: the title picks one
/ai-agency-skills:change-to-issue "docs: document the free-prose argument rule"
/change-to-issue                                           # no title — derive one
```

The plugin-qualified form is only needed when another command already claims the
bare name.

**A title was given.** It does two jobs:

- **It is the title.** Use it verbatim. The user has already decided how this
  will read in their tracker and is about to paste it into the field above your
  body — do not re-case it, tighten it, or bolt a conventional-commit prefix
  onto prose. If you think it is misleading, say so in your message and let them
  decide; do not quietly fix it.
- **It scopes the ticket.** Read the change through it. What the title names is
  the ticket; what it does not name goes under out-of-scope, or into a second
  ticket you offer to write. This is the case the argument exists for: a working
  tree holding two unrelated pieces of work, where the title says which one the
  user means.

If the title describes something the change does not actually do, stop and say
so rather than writing a body around it. A title and a body that disagree is the
one defect a reviewer cannot catch by reading the diff.

**No title was given.** Nothing changes — derive one from the change, as under
[Title and labels](#title-and-labels).

Either way the title is a scope statement, not source material. It never
substitutes for reading the change, and nothing enters the body because the
title implied it.

## What you are actually producing

A ticket written from a finished change has one reader in mind: a colleague who was
not there. They need to reach three conclusions on their own — why this exists, what
it does, and where they should push back — without reading the diff first.

That last part is what separates a ticket from a changelog. A changelog says what
moved. A ticket says what was decided and invites disagreement. If your draft could be
generated by `git log --oneline`, it has failed at the only job that mattered.

## Get the facts before writing anything

Write from the change, never from memory or from the summary you gave a moment ago —
your own recollection is exactly where invented details come from.

If the work happened in this conversation, you still have it; skim what you actually
changed rather than trusting the summary. Otherwise read it:

```bash
git status --short                    # what moved, including untracked files
git diff --stat                       # the shape and size of it
git diff                              # the substance (add --cached / a base ref as needed)
git log --oneline -15                 # house style for titles, and recent related work
```

For a branch, diff against the base: `git diff $(git merge-base HEAD main)...HEAD`.

Three things to extract while reading, because they are what the draft is built from:

1. **The problem.** What was impossible, broken, or annoying before this. Often visible
   in the diff only as absence — an endpoint that had no counterpart, a guard that was
   missing. State it in the user's own domain terms, not in terms of the patch.
2. **The arguable calls.** Places where a competent engineer would have chosen
   differently: a status code, a default, a refusal instead of a queue, a cap, a
   tradeoff between two bad options. These become the review surface.
3. **What is verified and what is not.** Tests run, checks passed, things you know are
   untested. Numbers here must be ones you actually observed.

If the diff mixes two unrelated pieces of work and no title was given, say so and offer
to split it into two tickets rather than quietly writing one that describes both. A
ticket that covers two things cannot be closed. If a title *was* given, it has already
chosen the half the user means: write that one, and list the other under out-of-scope.

## Match the shape to the size of the change

A heavy template stamped on a small fix reads as padding and trains people to skim
your tickets. Scale it:

| The change | The ticket |
|---|---|
| Typo, rename, version bump | Two or three sentences. Title, what and why. No headings. |
| Bug fix | Symptom → cause → fix → how it was verified. Include the repro. Skip "design decisions" unless the fix genuinely had a choice in it. |
| Feature or refactor with judgment calls | The full shape below. |
| Anything touching a contract other people depend on | The full shape, plus explicit before/after of the contract. |

## The full shape

Use these sections when the change earns them; drop any that would be empty. The order
matters more than the headings — problem before proposal, decisions before criteria.

```markdown
## Problem
Two to five sentences. What could not be done before, and why the obvious workaround
is not one. Concrete enough that someone hitting the same wall recognises it.

## Proposal
What now exists. A table of routes/commands/flags, or a short list of the new pieces
and what each is responsible for. This is the "what", kept brief — the diff has details.

## Design decisions worth reviewing
The heart of it. Each bullet: the call, then the reason, in one or two lines. Include
the ones you are least sure about — a decision nobody can find is a decision nobody
can correct. Where a choice follows from a single underlying fact, say the fact first
so the bullets read as consequences rather than a list of opinions.

## Config / migration
Only if operators or callers have to do something: new settings and defaults, a
breaking change, a data migration.

## Acceptance criteria
Observable behaviour, one per line, as checkboxes. Tick what is done. These should be
things a reviewer could check, not internal implementation notes.

## Out of scope / follow-ups
Unchecked boxes: the known gaps you deliberately left. Each should be plausible as its
own future ticket. This is where you are honest about what is not verified.

## Notes for the reviewer
Files touched, how it was tested, anything odd about the approach. Short.
```

## Honesty is the load-bearing part

The temptation in a ticket is to sound finished. Resist it in three specific places:

- **Numbers you did not observe.** "161 tests pass (was 139)" is worth writing only if
  you ran them. If you did not, write "test suite not run in this environment" and let
  the reviewer decide what that costs.
- **Verification you did not do.** If the code has never run against the real
  dependency — a live API, a real database, production data — that belongs in
  follow-ups as its own line, near the top. It is the single most useful thing a
  reviewer can learn from your draft.
- **Criteria you cannot check.** Do not tick a box because the code looks right.

A draft that admits a gap gets fixed. A draft that hides one gets merged.

## Title and labels

If the user supplied a title, that decision is already made — use it and skip to
labels.

Otherwise, read `git log --oneline` and match what the repo already does. If commits are
conventional (`feat(api): …`), title the issue the same way; if they are prose, use
prose. Keep it specific enough to be findable in a list of two hundred: name the thing
that changed, not the activity ("multi-turn session endpoints", not "API improvements").

For labels, prefer the repo's real ones — `gh label list` if the CLI is available and
authenticated — and otherwise suggest one or two obvious ones and say they are
guesses. Never invent a label taxonomy the project does not use.

## Handing it over

The user is going to paste this into a web form, so give them something pasteable:
write the body to a markdown file and deliver it, and put the title and labels in your
message rather than burying them in the file. Echo the title on its own line even when
they supplied it — they are filling two fields, and a title they can copy next to the
body beats one they have to scroll back for. Keep the surrounding commentary to a
couple of lines — point at the one or two decisions you most want challenged, and
mention if you think the change should have been split.

If they want it filed rather than drafted, `gh issue create --title … --body-file …`
does it — but show the draft first and let them approve. Filing is not undoable in the
way a draft is.

## Anti-patterns

- **The narrated diff.** "Added `SessionStore` to `sessions.py`. Updated `app.py` to
  wire it." Nobody can review that. Say what it makes possible and what it cost.
- **Fake gravitas on a small change.** Five headings for a one-line fix.
- **Decisions stated as facts.** "Concurrent requests return 409." — why? Without the
  reason there is nothing to agree or disagree with.
- **Acceptance criteria that restate the code.** "`SessionStore.close()` is called."
  A criterion is behaviour someone else can observe.
- **Silence about the unfinished parts.** Every real change has them.
